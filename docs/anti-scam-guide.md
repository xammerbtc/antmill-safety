# Anti-scam guide

Most crypto thefts do not start with a broken blockchain. They start with a rushed decision: a signed transaction, a pasted phrase, or a message that looks official.

This guide covers patterns we see often. It is education, not a complete catalog of every scam.

## 1. Drainer bots and fake “claim” apps

A common lure looks like a generous giveaway:

> “Connect your wallet and claim 15 SOL + 500 USDT.”
> “Login to unlock your airdrop.”
> “Open the app to verify your wallet.”

The site or Mini App may use familiar branding, a busy countdown, or a button that looks like a real wallet connect flow. After you approve the request, a malicious contract or a malicious signing prompt can empty the wallet. That class of theft is often called a **drainer**.

**What to do**

- Treat unsolicited “claim”, “verify”, or “unlock” wallet buttons as hostile until you have independently confirmed a real official campaign.
- Official teams almost never DM you first with a claim link.
- If a stranger’s bot, a comment, or a group mention offers a reward for connecting a wallet, walk away. Do not open the app, and do not “just check” it with a funded wallet.
- Never test a suspicious dapp with a wallet that holds funds or has token approvals.

A useful habit: keep a separate, nearly empty wallet for experiments. Even then, assume a malicious site can ask for dangerous approvals.

## 2. Seed phrases and recovery keys

Your seed phrase (recovery phrase), private key, or hardware-wallet backup is the wallet. Anyone who has it can take the assets, usually without a reverse button.

**Rules that do not have exceptions**

- Never type a seed phrase or private key into a website, a Telegram bot, a “support chat”, a Discord form, or a remote-help session.
- Never take a photo of it, store it in cloud screenshots, or paste it into email, notes synced to the cloud, or a password-reset form.
- No legitimate support agent, exchange, wallet vendor, or project team needs your seed phrase to “sync”, “fix”, or “verify” an account.
- If a page asks you to “import” or “validate” your phrase to receive a refund, it is a scam.
- Write a backup on paper or another offline method you control, and store it privately.

If you already entered a phrase on a site you do not trust, treat that wallet as compromised. Move remaining funds, if you still can, from a safe device to a **new** wallet whose phrase was never typed into that site. Then stop using the old wallet.

## 3. Fake support and fake admins

Scammers impersonate support in DMs, replies, and look-alike accounts.

Typical signs:

- A DM arrives right after you post “I need help” in a public group.
- The account uses the project name plus “Support”, “Admin”, or a ticket number, but it is not the official handle.
- They want you to move to WhatsApp, a private bot, or a “secure form”.
- They ask you to share a screenshot of a seed phrase, a seed plus a “verification code”, a seed in a “wallet connect” page, or remote-desktop access.
- They create urgency: “your funds will be lost in 15 minutes.”

**What to do**

- Official support is almost always something you start, from a link on the official site or the official in-app help, not from a surprise DM.
- Compare the username carefully. A single extra letter or a similar emoji is enough.
- In Telegram, open the project’s official profile or channel and use only the links published there.
- Never install remote-access software because “support” asked you to.

## 4. Other patterns worth recognizing

- **Address poisoning.** A dust transaction or a look-alike address appears in your history so you copy the wrong destination later. Always verify the full address, not just the first and last characters, preferably from your own address book.
- **Fake job or partnership DMs.** “We’ll pay you to test our app” or “connect a wallet to receive an advance.” Same rule: no seed, no funded-wallet connect, no urgency.
- **Malicious attachments and “connect to view the PDF” pages.** Prefer official clients and official sites. Do not install a “wallet update” from a chat file.
- **Approval farming.** A site asks you to approve unlimited token spending “just this once.” Review approvals periodically and revoke what you do not need, using a tool you already trust.

## 5. A 30-second pause

Before you sign, send, or connect, ask:

1. Did I type this official URL or username myself, or did I follow a chat link?
2. Is anyone rushing me?
3. Does this request need wallet access, a seed phrase, or a signature I do not understand?
4. If I walk away, do I actually lose anything real?

If the answer to (3) is yes and you cannot explain the request in plain language, do not approve it.
